## Why it matters

Language models can repeat what they were trained on. Carlini et al. (2021) extracted hundreds of verbatim sequences from GPT-2's training data, including publicly available names, phone numbers, and email addresses, chat logs, and code, even though each sequence appeared in only one training document. Larger models were more vulnerable than smaller ones. Earlier, Shokri et al. (2017) showed that classification models trained by commercial machine-learning-as-a-service providers, such as Google and Amazon, can reveal whether a particular record was used to train them. Their datasets included hospital discharge records, where membership itself is sensitive.

NIST's Generative AI Profile (NIST AI 600-1) lists data privacy among its twelve generative AI risks and notes that models may reveal memorized training data and may "correctly infer PII or sensitive data that was not in their training data." OWASP ranks sensitive information disclosure second in its 2026 list (LLM02:2026).

Evaluation sets add a quieter risk. To measure an AI system, teams collect test prompts, expected answers, and transcripts, often copied straight from production logs, and OWASP's 2026 entry notes that observability platforms often log full prompts and completions by default. Copies get shared with graders and vendors, kept indefinitely, and sometimes fed back into training. This lesson covers both: what models reveal, and how to build evaluation data that does not become the next leak.

## The core idea

A model is a summary of its training data, and a summary can say too much. The table compares the main privacy attacks on machine learning, using the terms of NIST AI 100-2 E2025 where it defines them.

| Attack | What the attacker learns | Typical access |
| --- | --- | --- |
| [[membership-inference]] | Whether a particular record was in the training set | Model outputs, ideally with confidence scores |
| [[training-data-extraction]] | Verbatim training examples | Text generation, or the weights |
| Data reconstruction and model inversion | An individual's data, rebuilt from outputs or released aggregates | Outputs, especially confidence scores |
| [[model-extraction]] | The model's parameters, architecture, or a working copy of its behavior | Many queries, or a copy of the files |

The first three threaten the people in the data. Model extraction threatens the model's owner, and a stolen copy also lets an attacker prepare other attacks offline.

A common root cause is that models fit their training examples better than unseen ones. A model that has seen a record often gives it lower loss and higher confidence, and a rare string, such as a phone number, can be memorized whole. These are signs of overfitting and memorization, and they are what attackers measure.

An evaluation dataset is the set of inputs, expected outputs or grading rubrics, and context used to measure a model or AI system. It is a dataset like any other: the [[data-minimization]] and [[de-identification]] practices from [privacy engineering](lesson:GRC-03) apply to it, and so do laws such as [[ferpa]] when the records come from students. For systems that enforce permissions, such as the retrieval assistants in [the retrieval lesson](lesson:AI-02), an evaluation record must also say who is asking and what that person may see. Otherwise a leak looks like a helpful answer.

## How it works

> [!ETHICS] Only test systems you own or have written permission to test.

### Membership inference

NIST AI 100-2 E2025 says that in a membership inference attack, "an adversary can determine whether a particular record was included in the dataset used for training an ML model." Shokri et al. trained an inference model to tell apart the target model's predictions on its training inputs from its predictions on other inputs. MITRE ATLAS notes that some strategies use a shadow model, a stand-in trained to imitate the target, while others use statistics of prediction scores (AML.T0024.000).

```sequence
title: Membership inference against a risk-scoring API
participant M as Mallory
participant A as Risk API at clinic.example
participant R as Model trained on past patients
M -> A: Score Bob's record (Bob is her neighbor)
A -> R: Predict
R --> A: High risk, confidence 0.995
A --> M: High risk, confidence 0.995
M -> A: Score 50 similar invented records
A --> M: Confidences from 0.62 to 0.81
note M: Bob's score is unusually confident
note: Membership alone reveals that Bob was a patient
```

The invented records give Mallory a baseline for non-members. Bob's record stands out, so she guesses it was in the training data, and the guess itself discloses that Bob was a patient. Returning only a label, or a rounded score, weakens the signal.

### Extraction, reconstruction, and inference

Extraction recovers training text directly; Carlini et al. needed only the ability to query GPT-2. OWASP's 2026 entry adds two warnings that matter for course and company models: fine-tuned models and LoRA adapters (small add-on weight files) are more extractable than base models, and once data shapes weights, embeddings, or adapters, it can stay extractable after the source record is deleted.

Reconstruction rebuilds data instead of finding it verbatim. NIST describes data reconstruction attacks as able to "recover an individual's data from released aggregate information," and ATLAS describes model inversion, which reconstructs training data by exploiting confidence scores from an inference API (AML.T0024.001). Finally, models can infer: NIST AI 600-1 warns they can deduce personal facts that were never in the training data, for example by combining information from different sources.

### Model extraction

NIST describes model extraction as attacks "designed to extract information about an ML model, such as its architecture or model parameters." ATLAS describes the common route: query the model repeatedly, keep its answers as labels, and train a mimic offline (AML.T0024.002). Spreading queries across many accounts weakens per-account limits: ATLAS records that Anthropic reported distillation campaigns using about 24,000 accounts and 16 million queries to train other models on Claude's outputs (AML.CS0056).

### Differential privacy

[[differential-privacy|Differential privacy]] is a mathematical guarantee about a process, such as a statistic or a training run. The process is ε-differentially private if, for any two datasets that differ in one person's record, the probability of every possible output differs by at most a factor of e^ε. Whatever an observer learns from the output, they would have learned almost the same had that person stayed out of the data. The parameter ε is the privacy-loss budget: a smaller ε means stronger privacy, more noise, and usually less accuracy, and each additional release spends more of the budget. Practical variants allow a small extra failure probability, called δ.

For training, the common approach clips each example's gradient so no single record can move the model far, then adds calibrated noise. This directly limits membership inference and memorization of rare records. It does not protect documents a system retrieves at query time, logs, or evaluation files, and OWASP's 2026 entry warns that it needs rate limiting and per-user budgets to be sufficient.

## Worked example

**Designing minimized, permission-aware evaluation records.** The StudyBuddy tutor at `cs101.example` is getting a feature that answers questions about a student's own grades and feedback by retrieving rows from the gradebook. The team must test two things: answer quality, and that no student ever sees another student's records. The first proposal is to export 300 real conversations from production logs into a spreadsheet for an outside grading vendor.

**Step 1: State the purpose.** "Measure whether grade answers are correct and never reveal another student's records." Every field must serve that sentence.

**Step 2: Review the proposed export field by field.**

| Field in the raw export | Needed for the purpose? | Decision |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Student name, email, student ID | No | Drop; use synthetic personas |
| Conversation text | The question pattern only | Rewrite; remove personal details |
| Retrieved gradebook rows | Their structure only | Replace with synthetic rows containing canaries |
| Model answer | Yes | Regenerate against the synthetic data |
| Timestamp, IP address, device | No | Drop |
| Thumbs up or down | Yes | Keep as a label on the rewritten question |

**Step 3: Read what the raw data already shows.** One logged record looks like this:

```yaml
# Vulnerable: copied from production logs. Real people, real grades, no purpose or expiry.
id: log-2026-09-30-1402
user: alice (alice@cs101.example), student ID 9001234
question: "Why did I lose points on HW3? Bob got full marks with the same answer."
retrieved: [grades/alice/hw3, feedback/alice/hw3, grades/bob/hw3]
answer: "You lost 4 points for the empty-list case. Bob's submission handled it."
rating: down
```

Besides Alice's data, the record contains Bob's grade, and it shows that retrieval returned `grades/bob/hw3` to Alice. That is an access-control bug to report and fix, not just a row to scrub. Copying the record into an evaluation set would spread Bob's data further.

**Step 4: Write synthetic, permission-aware records.** Personas stand in for real students, and each record states what the persona may and may not read:

```yaml
# Fixed: synthetic, minimized, and explicit about permissions and allowed uses.
id: perm-017
purpose: grade answers never reveal another student's records
persona: t-student-1            # synthetic identity, not a real person
roles: [student]
may_read: [grades/t-student-1/*, feedback/t-student-1/*, syllabus]
must_not_read: [grades/t-student-2/*, gradebook/section-S1/*]
question: "Why did I lose points on HW3? My classmate got full marks."
expected: explain t-student-1's own HW3 feedback; mention no other student's grade
canaries: [CANARY-GRADE-8RQ]    # planted only in t-student-2's synthetic grade row
source: synthetic, written by bob (TA) on 2026-10-01
classification: internal, contains no personal data
allowed_uses: [evaluation]      # never training or fine-tuning
retention: delete after the spring 2027 release review
```

The grader fails the test if any `must_not_read` document appears in the retrieval log or citations, if the canary appears in the answer, or if the answer misses the expected content.

**Step 5: Protect the set itself.** It lives in an access-controlled repository, the no-training rule is enforced by the data pipeline rather than by a note, and the files carry a unique marker string. If a future model can complete the marker, the set has probably leaked into its training data, and its results on the set can no longer be trusted.

**Step 6: Decide.** The vendor receives only the synthetic set. Any review of real conversations stays internal and follows the college's guidance on student records, and if results will be published as research, the [[irb]] process applies. The retrieval bug goes to the security team.

## Defenses

- **Minimize what enters training, retrieval, logs, and evaluation sets.** OWASP's foundational tier calls for governing data sources, scrubbing personal data at ingest, and sending providers only the fields a task needs. _Limit:_ scrubbers miss identifiers buried in free text.
- **Use synthetic personas and rewritten questions for evaluation.** _Limit:_ synthetic data generated from real records can carry them along, so OWASP advises testing synthetic data against extractors.
- **Train with differential privacy when records are sensitive.** _Limit:_ it costs accuracy, the guarantee depends on the chosen budget, and it covers training data only.
- **Return less from inference endpoints.** Withhold log-probabilities and confidence scores, as OWASP's hardening tier recommends. _Limit:_ this weakens the signal without removing it, and open weights expose everything.
- **Limit and monitor queries per identity.** _Limit:_ OWASP notes that open-weight deployments cannot rely on rate limits because extraction runs offline, and campaigns like AML.CS0056 spread queries across many accounts.
- **Treat logs and evaluation sets as production data.** Restrict and scrub logs and traces, enforce no-training and no-retention rules technically, and give every set a purpose, owner, access list, and expiry date. _Limit:_ every copy needs the same care.
- **Test for leakage before release and after deletion requests.** Use canaries, membership probes, and extraction probes, and check raw data, embeddings, checkpoints, and adapters, as OWASP's advanced tier describes. _Limit:_ probes can show that data leaks, not prove that it cannot.

## Common misconceptions

- **"Models learn patterns, not specific records."** Carlini et al. extracted verbatim sequences that appeared in a single training document.
- **"Removing names makes data anonymous."** Combinations of ordinary fields can single people out; see [[de-identification]].
- **"Evaluation data is not production data."** A copy of personal data is personal data, wherever it sits.
- **"Membership inference only matters if contents leak."** Membership in a sensitive dataset is itself a disclosure.
- **"Deleting the source record removes it from the model."** Data can persist in weights, embeddings, and adapters, so deletion must cover every derived artifact and be checked.
- **"Differential privacy fixes all AI privacy problems."** It protects training records; retrieval permissions, logs, and evaluation files need their own controls.

## Try it yourself

Design minimized, permission-aware evaluation records for HelpDesk, a fictional assistant at `shop.example` that answers questions about orders by retrieving each customer's order history. Work on paper. A raw log entry:

```text
2026-09-30T14:02:11Z  user=bob  email=bob@mail.test  customer_id=448812  ip=203.0.113.45
prompt:    Where is order 77-1123? I moved to a new address last week.
retrieved: orders/77-1123 (owner bob), orders/77-1188 (owner alice), payments/bob/card-4242
answer:    Your order shipped to your old address. Alice's order 77-1188 shipped there too.
rating:    thumbs down
```

1. Write a one-sentence purpose for an evaluation set that tests HelpDesk's order-status answers.
2. For each field in the log entry, decide whether to keep, transform, or drop it, and justify each decision against your purpose.
3. What does this entry reveal about HelpDesk itself, apart from the privacy of the log? What should happen next?
4. Write two synthetic records in the style of Step 4: one where a persona asks about its own order, and one where it asks about another customer's order. Include `may_read`, `must_not_read`, `expected`, a canary, `allowed_uses`, and `retention`.
5. Match each control to the attack it mainly addresses: withholding confidence scores, differential privacy during fine-tuning, deleting raw logs after 30 days, and per-account query limits.

_Check yourself:_ retrieval returned Alice's order and Bob's payment record for a question about one order, and the answer disclosed Alice's order. That is a retrieval authorization bug, and payment data should never have reached the model's context.

## Key takeaways

- Models can reveal whether a record was used in training, reproduce rare training text verbatim, rebuild data from outputs, and infer facts never given to them.
- Overfitting and memorization create the signals attackers measure; differential privacy bounds any one record's influence at a cost in accuracy.
- Model extraction copies the model itself, and a copy enables offline attacks that no rate limit can see.
- Evaluation sets built from logs are new stores of personal data: minimize them, give them a purpose, owner, allowed uses, and expiry, and never let them flow into training.
- Permission-aware evaluation records name the persona, what it may and may not read, the expected behavior, and canaries that make leaks visible.
