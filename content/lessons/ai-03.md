## Why it matters

A language model can write a confident, well-organized paragraph that is false. NIST's Generative AI Profile (NIST AI 600-1) calls this risk confabulation: generative systems "generate and confidently present erroneous or false content in response to prompts." The everyday word is [[hallucination]]. It is not a rare glitch. When Min and colleagues broke model-written biographies into individual facts and checked each one against a reference source, ChatGPT's biographies in their 2023 study scored 58%: on average, only 58% of their facts were supported.

False output becomes a security problem when someone, or something, acts on it. OWASP's 2026 list ranks misinformation seventh (LLM07:2026) and puts the danger plainly: "The core risk is that the incorrect output is trusted and acted upon." MITRE ATLAS records a concrete case (AML.CS0022): researchers found that coding assistants can recommend software packages that do not exist. An attacker who notices an invented name can register it with malicious code, and developers who trust the recommendation install it. ATLAS adds that different models have been shown to produce the same invented names, so one malicious registration can catch users of several assistants.

As assistants move from answering questions to triggering actions, the cost of a false claim grows. An agent that wrongly believes a backup succeeded, or that a customer is verified, can delete data or release money.

## The core idea

Start with the assumptions. A language model is trained to produce likely text, not true text. NIST notes that confabulations are "a natural result of the way generative models are designed," because outputs approximate the statistical patterns of the training data. Truth and likelihood tend to agree for common facts and can come apart for rare ones: a specific date, a court case, a configuration flag, a package name, or a statistic.

So treat every factual claim as a hypothesis that needs evidence. [[grounding|Grounding]] means tying each claim to a source the reader can check. [[retrieval-augmented-generation|Retrieval]] helps by putting relevant documents in front of the model, but it does not guarantee faithful use. The model can cite a passage that does not say what it claims, blend two sources, use an outdated document, or fill a gap from memory and attach a citation anyway.

That gives three separate questions for every claim:

1. **Is it supported by the cited source?** Does the passage actually say this?
2. **Is the source authoritative and current?** Is it the latest policy, or a draft from 2023?
3. **Is it true?** A claim can be supported by a bad source, or true but unsupported.

An analogy: footnotes in a term paper are only as good as what the cited page says, which is why careful readers spot-check them. The danger of [[overreliance]] is that people stop spot-checking once the footnotes look professional.

## How it works

### Why models get things wrong

- **Long-tail facts.** Rare details are learned weakly, but the model still produces something plausible.
- **False premises.** A question such as "Why was the midterm moved?" invites an explanation even if nothing moved.
- **Stale knowledge.** Training data has a cutoff, and retrieval indexes go out of date.
- **Retrieval failures.** The search returns the wrong document, an old version, or conflicting versions.
- **Lossy summaries.** OWASP lists misleading summaries that drop constraints, exceptions, or timestamps among its common examples.
- **Adversarial input.** Planted documents and [[prompt-injection|injected instructions]] can steer answers, and ATLAS lists manipulated citations as a technique (AML.T0067.000): the wrong citation, a made-up one, or a real citation attached to the adversary's data.

Confidence is not a reliable signal in any of these cases. OWASP observes that "humans and systems often treat fluent, confident, or well-structured outputs as authoritative."

### Labels for evidence support

| Label         | Meaning                                                   | Example against "Labs may be submitted up to 48 hours late" |
| ------------- | --------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------- |
| Supported     | The passage states or directly implies the claim          | "Late labs are accepted for two days"                       |
| Contradicted  | The passage addresses the claim and disagrees             | "Labs may be submitted 72 hours late"                       |
| Not addressed | The passage is silent, so the claim is unsupported        | "Late labs need instructor approval"                        |
| Bad citation  | The cited source does not exist or is not the one quoted | A section number the syllabus does not have                 |

Labeling works best on atomic claims, each stating a single fact. FActScore, the method Min et al. used for the 58% result, splits text into such claims and measures the share a knowledge source supports; the authors also built an automated estimator that they report has an error rate below 2%.

### From false claim to security incident

> [!ETHICS] Only test systems you own or have written permission to test.

The package case shows how a hallucination turns into a supply-chain attack:

```sequence
title: A hallucinated package becomes a supply-chain attack
participant M as Mallory
participant C as Coding assistant
participant P as Package registry
participant D as Developer
M -> C: Asks many coding questions
C --> M: Answers that name packages that do not exist
M -> P: Registers one invented name with malicious code
D -> C: How do I parse this file format?
C --> D: Install the same invented package
D -> P: Install the package
P --> D: Mallory's malicious code
note: Defense: verify packages before installing
```

Mallory never attacks the assistant itself. ATLAS calls her first step discovering hallucinations (AML.T0062) and her second publishing hallucinated entities (AML.T0060). The assistant's false claim does the rest, because the developer trusts it. Other routes from false claims to harm include agents that act on a wrong belief about the world, false troubleshooting steps planted on a forum and then repeated by an assistant, and fabricated citations that lend false authority.

## Worked example

**Checking source grounding on a reviewed question set.** StudyBuddy, the fictional tutor at `cs101.example`, answers course questions with citations to the syllabus and readings. Before the semester, the instructor, Alice, and her teaching assistant, Bob, measure how well its claims are grounded. They test a copy of the tutor with the real course documents but no student data.

**Step 1: Build a reviewed question set.** Alice and Bob write 40 questions and, for each, a reviewed answer with the exact supporting passage. The set mixes four kinds:

| Kind          | Count | Example                                     | Correct behavior                   |
| ------------- | ----- | ------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------- |
| Answerable    | 26    | "What is the late policy for labs?"         | Answer with a supporting citation  |
| Unanswerable  | 6     | "What is the room number for the final?"    | Say the sources do not cover it    |
| False premise | 4     | "Why was the midterm moved to November?"    | Correct the premise                |
| Conflicting   | 4     | An old and a new version of one policy      | Use the current version, or flag it |

**Step 2: Run and record.** For each question, they save the answer, its citations, and the passages retrieval actually returned.

**Step 3: Split answers into atomic claims and label them.** One answer reads: "Yes, you can submit Lab 3 late. Labs can be submitted up to 72 hours late with a 10% daily penalty [syllabus 4]. Late work needs instructor approval [syllabus 4]." The cited passage says labs may be submitted up to 48 hours late with a 10% penalty per day, except Lab 5.

| Claim                                  | Label against syllabus section 4 |
| -------------------------------------- | -------------------------------- |
| Lab 3 can be submitted late            | Supported                        |
| The limit is 72 hours                  | Contradicted                     |
| The penalty is 10% per day             | Supported                        |
| Late work needs instructor approval    | Not addressed                    |

The answer looks well sourced, yet half its claims are wrong or unsupported, and the same citation sits on supported, contradicted, and unsupported claims alike.

**Step 4: Compute metrics.** Two reviewers label every claim independently, then resolve disagreements; their agreement rate is reported too. Suppose the illustrative totals are 123 of 150 claims supported (82%), 9 contradicted, 18 not addressed; correct abstention on 4 of 6 unanswerable questions; and corrected premises on 2 of 4 false-premise questions.

**Step 5: Interpret honestly.** A Wilson 95% interval for 123 of 150 runs from about 75% to 87%, and the true uncertainty is wider because claims from the same answer are correlated. The abstention and premise results rest on six and four questions, far too few to estimate a rate; treat them as warnings. The numbers describe this question set, this model version, and these settings, so Alice reruns the set after every model, prompt, or document change.

**Step 6: Act on the findings.** The contradicted claims cluster on numbers, so Alice adds an automatic check that every number in an answer appears in a cited passage. Conflicting versions caused two errors, so old syllabus drafts leave the index.

## Defenses

- **Ground answers in authoritative, current sources.** Index the official versions, retire drafts, and prefer the newest dated document. _Limit:_ retrieval can still return the wrong passage.
- **Check citations automatically.** Require claim-level citations, confirm each one points to a retrieved passage, and test whether the passage supports the claim, for example by matching numbers and names or with an entailment model. _Limit:_ automated checkers make their own mistakes, so validate them against human labels.
- **Let the assistant abstain.** Reward "the sources do not say" and measure abstention directly. _Limit:_ an assistant tuned to abstain too often becomes useless; track both rates.
- **Claim, check, act.** OWASP's 2026 list recommends separating generation from execution: code verifies any claim that gates an action against the system of record before acting. _Limit:_ it covers only claims you identify as gating actions.
- **Verify generated dependencies.** Check that a package exists, has a history, and is on an allowlist or internal mirror before installing; see [software dependencies](lesson:CLD-06). _Limit:_ attackers can age a malicious package to look established.
- **Design for calibrated trust.** Show sources and quoted passages next to claims, and distinguish verified facts from assumptions, as OWASP recommends. _Limit:_ busy readers still skim.
- **Keep humans in the loop for high-stakes answers** and audit a sample regularly. _Limit:_ reviewers tire and start trusting the system too.

## Common misconceptions

- **"Retrieval eliminates hallucination."** It supplies evidence, but the model can still misread, blend, or ignore it.
- **"A citation means the claim is supported."** Only checking the passage tells you that.
- **"Confident wording signals accuracy."** Models are confident whether right or wrong.
- **"Ask the model how sure it is."** A model's own confidence is a weak signal; OWASP recommends groundedness and consistency checks rather than relying on it.
- **"Hallucination is only a quality issue."** Invented packages, forged citations, and agents acting on false beliefs are security problems.
- **"One evaluation run settles it."** Results shift with model versions, prompts, documents, and sampling.

## Try it yourself

Check a model's claims against the cited evidence. Work on paper.

```text
SOURCE [S1] Library Wi-Fi guide, updated 2026-08-01
  "Guests connect to the LibGuest network. Guest sessions last 8 hours.
   Students use eduroam with their campus credentials."
SOURCE [S2] Password policy, updated 2025-01-15
  "Passwords must be at least 12 characters. Passwords expire every 365 days."
SOURCE [S3] Password policy, updated 2026-07-01
  "Passwords must be at least 15 characters. Passwords do not expire unless
   there is evidence of compromise."

QUESTION: How long must my password be, and how often must I change it?
ANSWER: Your password must be at least 12 characters [S3] and must be changed
every 365 days [S2]. You can reset it while connected to LibGuest [S1].
```

1. Split the answer into atomic claims.
2. Label each claim against its own citation: supported, contradicted, or not addressed.
3. Which claim is supported by its citation but still wrong for the student? Why?
4. Compute citation precision: the share of citations that support the claim they are attached to.
5. Propose two controls that would have prevented these errors, and say what each would miss.

**Optional, on your own computer.** Give a local open-weight model the three sources and the question, run it five times, and label every answer the same way. Note how much the answers vary between runs.

## Key takeaways

- Language models produce likely text, not verified text, so every factual claim needs evidence.
- A citation is a pointer, not proof: check claim by claim whether the cited passage supports, contradicts, or ignores the claim.
- Misinformation becomes a security problem when people or agents act on it, as hallucinated packages show.
- Measure grounding on a reviewed question set that includes unanswerable, false-premise, and conflicting cases, and report uncertainty and the set's limits.
- Verify claims that gate actions in code, against the system of record.

## Go deeper

- Min et al. (2023) introduced FActScore, which splits long answers into atomic facts and scores the share a knowledge source supports. Open question: how far can automated checkers be trusted on specialized documents, and how should we validate the validator?
- NIST AI 600-1 treats confabulation as one of twelve generative AI risks and lists suggested actions, organized by the AI Risk Management Framework's functions, that address it.
- OWASP's 2026 entry on misinformation (LLM07:2026) extends the topic to agents, including incorrect state inference, cross-agent propagation, and fabricated task completion.
- MITRE ATLAS techniques AML.T0062 and AML.T0060 describe how attackers discover and register hallucinated entities, a useful threat model for any assistant that recommends software, links, or contacts.
